## 1. “Unlimited” is advertised, but a fair-use restriction is buried on another page

`medium impact` · `Ambiguity` · `high confidence`

Your commercial pages advertise unlimited marketing sends, while a separate page qualifies usage with a fair-use or acceptable-use restriction. A customer deciding on price never sees the qualifier; a customer who hits it experiences it as a broken promise.

**What your pricing says:** Unlimited marketing sends (no specific plan)
> Pick Marketing Platform for $29/mo for up to 5,000 Active Users * , get unlimited marketing sends on paid plans. Add Bento Chat for $30/mo, or use Transactional Email on its own.
> — [Simple plans, no hidden fees.](https://bentonow.com/pricing)

**What another page says:** Usage is subject to a fair-use / acceptable-use restriction
> An Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) is a set of rules defined by an ESP or ISP that governs what type of content and behavior is allowed on their network.
> — [Email Marketing Glossary](https://bentonow.com/terms)

*Worth knowing: Fair-use clauses are standard practice. The issue is placement and wording, not the existence of the clause.*

**These are the promises your customers can see. Do your billing system and product deliver the same thing?**

This audit only reads what is published on your website. It cannot see what your billing system actually meters, what your product actually enforces, or what your customer records actually entitle people to. In most companies those three answers have drifted apart quietly, and the public pages are the only place the drift is visible from outside. If the contradictions above are news to you, the more expensive question is what else is out of step behind them.
