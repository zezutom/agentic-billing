Your headline says you charge for jobs done; your FAQ says scheduling an appointment is chargeable on its own

The whole positioning of your pricing page rests on one sentence: legacy EHRs charge for access, you charge for work completed. Your own FAQ then defines a billable session as generating an AI note or simply scheduling a session with a client, and adds that a telehealth call counts even when no note is produced. A therapist who schedules forty appointments and writes twenty notes pays for forty. That may be entirely reasonable given the cap, and you explain the logic in the same answer, but it is not what the headline led them to expect, and this is the number that determines their bill.

What one page says
You charge for work completed, not for access to the platform
“Legacy EHRs charge for access. We charge for jobs done.”
https://www.upheal.io/pricing
What another page says
A session is billable when an appointment is scheduled, whether or not a note is ever produced
“A session is counted each time you generate an AI note or schedule a session with a client”
https://www.upheal.io/pricing
Why this is not just wording: Scheduling an appointment is an act of access rather than a job done, so the billing rule charges for precisely the thing the headline says you do not charge for.
What would make this a non-issue: The $69 monthly cap bounds the damage, and the same FAQ answer explains the reasoning: the per-session fee covers the whole platform, not just the note. A clause on the headline claim would close the gap without weakening it.

Your free insurance-billing launch offer is advertised without restriction and limited to solo practices only in the small print

Your pricing page states the insurance billing fees and marks them free until the end of September, with no qualification. The insurance billing feature page repeats the launch offer twice more, in the hero and in the pricing block, again without qualification. The restriction appears once, inside the answer to "What does it cost?": solo practices only. Group practices are exactly the customers who submit enough claims for $0.30 apiece to matter, and they are the ones your pricing page leaves with the wrong number in their model.

What would make this a non-issue: Adding four words to the pricing page and the two banners would resolve this entirely. Note also that the restriction sentence contains a typo, which suggests it was added late rather than written alongside the offer.

Your Canadian pricing page quotes card fees in Canadian dollars and insurance billing fees in US dollars

The Canadian version of your pricing page converts carefully: C$1 per session, capped at C$69 a month, and card transactions at 2.9% + C$0.30. In the same feature list, insurance billing is quoted as $0.30 per claim and $0.15 per eligibility check, with no C. Two of your dated offers have no year on them, and one of them describes a date that has passed

Your managed insurance billing page invites people onto a waitlist for a closed beta that opens in August 2026. It is now September. Separately, the free-claims launch offer runs "till the end of September" on four different pages without a year attached, so a reader in any September cannot tell whether they have four weeks or missed it last year. Neither is serious on its own; together they make time-limited offers on the site hard to trust, which is the opposite of what a launch offer is for.

How to read these

Likely contradiction — Two public statements that cannot both be true.
Ambiguity — Both statements can be true, but a customer cannot tell what they get.
Possibly out of date — Content that looks left over from an earlier version of your packaging.
Missing information — Something that changes the deal, published somewhere the buyer will not look.
